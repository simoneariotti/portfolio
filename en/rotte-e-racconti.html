Personal projectEditorial systemRotte e RaccontiSince 2026, ongoing

Rules do not live in the head of whoever invented them.

A two-person editorial project that produces like a team, because the system that governs it is written down, versioned and hierarchical.

01 · Context

He comes back with the folders. I build the system that holds them.

Rotte e Racconti documents real journeys through photography, aerial footage and historical-geographical reconstruction. The division of labour is sharp and declared: the visual material in the diaries is Emanuele's, who travels the routes and documents them. The rest is mine: positioning, information architecture, design system, product, content pipeline, development and publishing. I receive folders of photos, a diary in plain text, a video track, and I return published travel monographs.

What Rotte e Racconti is

The positioning lives in a manifesto: we do not sell experiences, we document places. The payoff is Inhabiting a place before reaching it. From there follows a list of prohibitions that removes almost every standard lever of a travel project up front: no generated images, no stock photography, no third-party material, no clickbait headlines, no banners, and never document a place you have not been to. A project built like this lives on accumulated credibility, not on bought traffic.

02 · The problem

Perfect rules on the text, no rules on anything else.

After three published trips the project had a solid editorial standard and nothing else. No criterion for judging an interface: the manifesto listed the patterns in use without saying what made them right. No rules on appearance, and the five documented visual values were in fact wrong, because they described the hand-written map components rather than the design of the site. The reason is trivial and instructive: code can be read, design cannot.

Above these sat the problem that contains them all. Three conflicting sources declared three different character limits, and nobody knew which one held. The rules were not badly written: they were written in places that did not talk to each other, and for the rest they lived in the head of whoever invented them.

A two-person project running on case-by-case decisions does not break straight away. It degrades by the third trip.

03 · The reasoning

A hierarchy, not an archive of documents.

The problem with a body of rules is not writing them, it is knowing which one wins when two contradict each other. I defined five documents with declared precedence: the manifesto prevails over everything, the editorial standard, the product standard and the design system rank equally over different perimeters, the decision log is not normative, and the archives of individual trips yield to the standards.

Three properties make it usable rather than decorative.

  • Disjoint perimeters. A rule about navigation does not live in the editorial standard, a colour value does not live in the context file. Every rule has one home only, so there are never two versions of the same rule slowly drifting apart.
  • The decision log does not decide. It keeps what we know separate from what we are assuming. If a question is tracked there, the correct answer is that it is open, not an improvisation.
  • Versions with a reason. Whoever changes a document updates the version and notes why. An unversioned change is a lost change.

The effect I had not expected is that the body of rules corrects itself. The context file states in writing that a prohibition it contained was its own mistake, and that the visual values it hosted were wrong. A system that can admit its own errors at the exact point where it made them does not accumulate silent debt.

The catalogue and the magic number

The two most instructive design decisions are the same move applied to different matters.

The layout. On the Japan diary it broke: vertical photos in horizontal slots, two hundred pixel differences between adjacent photos, gaps that read as errors. The diagnosis was not aesthetic. The generator assigned space by position, the third photo goes in the tall slot, and it worked only because on Lanzarote verticals were sixteen per cent and always fell last. It had mistaken a regularity of the material for a rule. On Japan verticals are sixty-nine per cent and fall anywhere.

I removed the catalogue of layouts and put a single rule in its place: within a row the photos share the same height and together they fill the full width. From there the layout is computed rather than chosen. Verified at eight widths from 320 to 1920 pixels across two diaries: no cropping, every row full. Before, a three-by-two photo lost eleven per cent of its width to fit a four-by-three slot.

The stops on the map. The standard imposed eight to twelve stops per trip, and four diaries out of five were ignoring it already. Measured on the published site: twelve stops over four days, fifteen over five, thirty-five over twelve, forty-two over eleven. A fixed ceiling does not tell a four-day trip from a twelve-day one, and applied to Turkey, nine days and 2,450 kilometres, it would have produced the emptiest map on the site for the longest itinerary. The rule now scales: two or three stops per documented day, each with an associated photo and a coordinate from a public source. If either is missing, the stop is not placed.

A rule almost nobody follows is not a rule being ignored. It is the wrong rule.

04 · The research

Designing the test, not just reading its results.

It is the only point in the project where a product decision comes from external evidence rather than internal judgement, so the whole operation is worth telling and not just the outcome. A pre-launch study designed, run and analysed by me: twenty-nine people, free navigation followed by a sixteen-question survey, four of them open.

Free navigation rather than tasks, deliberately. I wanted to know where they got lost on their own, not whether they could carry out a task I had written myself. And the moment was pre-launch, because that is when changing costs little.

The questions are built to triangulate, not to confirm. The same possible fracture in positioning is measured three times with different instruments. If the site communicated one thing only, the three answers would converge.

  • 01How would you describe it to a friendOpen question. About twelve people describe a tool for planning, about nine a documentary narrative.
  • 02How do you read the payoffClosed question. Fifteen read immersion, eleven preparation, three do not understand it.
  • 03How is it different from other contentClosed question. Eleven out of twenty-nine say it has a clear identity, eight answer literally that it is different but they could not say how.

Three different instruments, the same split, similar proportions: this is not random ambiguity, the site communicates two things at once. And a project the reader cannot describe generates no word of mouth, which is the only channel consistent with a project that refuses the algorithm.

Deciding which data deserves to be used

The sample was one of proximity: people who know the project or the people behind it. One answer mentions the trips by name, another jokes about firing me. Rather than ignoring the limit or half-ignoring it, I turned it into a rule for using the dataset, written into the report before the results.

  • Rating scales. Inflated by politeness: not to be used as a measure of quality.
  • Recommendation score. Friendly sample: never quoted in commercial material.
  • The question on what you would remove, with nineteen answers out of twenty-nine saying nothing. It asks people to criticise, so it says nothing about completeness.
  • Open answers with specific content. Criticising takes effort, so they are the only signal worth acting on.

Differences between subgroups were calculated and discarded: with eight to fifteen people per cell they are noise. The open answers were coded by hand and counted per person across three cross-referenced questions, not per mention: counting mentions would have given more weight to whoever writes more. The report also states what the dataset cannot say, for instance how a reader who does not know the project reacts, because the sample contained not a single one.

What it turned into

The main result: thirteen people out of twenty-nine report friction on navigation, the map or initial orientation. Everyone found the diaries, but those who managed did so by trial and error. The cost is invisible in this dataset precisely because the sample was motivated to reach the end: a cold reader leaves.

From there comes the product standard, which did not exist before, with the distinction that makes it applicable. The absence of aggressive prompts is register, the absence of any indication of what to do is friction. Restraint is register, interactive elements indistinguishable from static ones are friction. Slowness as a reading rhythm is register, slowness as time lost working out the interface is friction.

Design serves the material. An interface the reader cannot use is not serving the material: it is hiding it.

Alongside the standard came thirteen tracked debts, each with a declared origin and a status verified on the live site, two strategic decisions kept open rather than settled on instinct, and something that is always forgotten: a list of things not to touch. Photographic quality, legibility, the day-by-day structure, the absence of advertising, the restraint of the register. After a test the temptation to improve everything is the most concrete risk, and confirmations deserve protecting as carefully as criticisms.

One criticism I did not turn into a mandate: four people ask for more authorial personality. On a friendly sample that is noise, and it does not license touching the third-person rule. But it is the only response that attacks a system decision rather than a detail, so I recorded it for what it is: the measure of what that choice costs, not proof that it is wrong.

The next round, already designed

The report closes by stating that this instrument has exhausted what it could say, and with which methods to answer what remains: cold readers with real tasks, observed while they carry them out; traffic measurement before any redesign; a targeted test on the text with a recall after twenty-four hours, to separate two hypotheses the survey cannot tell apart, namely whether the text is too short or whether it lacks memorable anchors. And it states what not to repeat: rating scales and recommendation scores on a friendly sample, because they take up space and produce no signal.

The second front: questioning the source

Alongside the reader test there is a less visible and equally structured practice: the questions to the photographer. When comparing text, photos and metadata produces a doubt only someone who was there can settle, the doubt is not resolved by estimate. It becomes an entry in a versioned document, ordered by the cost of not knowing, with what is known, what is missing and what changes on the page depending on the answer.

One example stands for all of them: the times recorded in two photos, one on the ridge and one forty minutes of hairpins away twenty-three minutes later, make it impossible for the sunset to have been watched from the lighthouse. Until the answer comes, the text does not say so. It is the same method as the reader test applied to a different source: gather evidence, declare what is not known, and do not fill the gap with an assumption.

05 · Result

The system held across six trips and a change of platform.

  • 6trips documented, from four to twelve days
  • 137geolocated stops, each with a photo and a verified coordinate
  • 366photos published, in 1,332 derivatives across three resolutions
  • 5versioned normative documents, with declared precedence

The material was not uniform: from sixteen to sixty-nine per cent vertical photos, from four to twelve days per trip. That is why the system was genuinely put to the test.

The site was rebuilt outside the original platform, with no framework: a static generator where the content is the single source of truth. A text file per trip holds the facts, the code holds the rules, and the distinction is deliberate: a rule inside the content would have to be rewritten for every trip, a fact inside the code would be forgotten. The page is never touched by hand: you change the source and regenerate.

The defect that made the rebuild necessary is measured, not feared: two captions from the Scotland diary had stayed inside Japanese days, through a component reused without replacing its content.

Accessibility verified on the published site: contrast ratios from 5.9 to 18.7 to one, visible focus on every interactive element, no primary action left to hover, behaviour checked with reduced motion.

06 · What stays open

A case study that closes everything is a case study badly told.

  • No traffic measurement installed. Every statement about the real behaviour of readers is an assumption. It is the decision that could be closed first and I have not closed it yet: it is the debt that costs me most.
  • No mechanism for returning. Every visit is terminal. Fifteen people out of twenty-nine were flagging it already, and the decision is still open.
  • Map attribution missing on the published site. A legal debt, not an aesthetic one. Tracked, not yet settled.
  • Tension between reader and client. The project is built as editorial property for readers and would be monetisable as a content studio for companies: two opposite optimisations. Tracked as a decision that cannot be resolved in one step.

A principle that emerged

A catalogue of cases and a magic number are the same thing: a rule that is not there yet. The work is finding the one that generates them.
